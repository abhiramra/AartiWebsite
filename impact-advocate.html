Most of this comes down to information

“Anna ki attu, naaku thittu…” A Telugu folk song — a girl sings of going hungry while the boy is given the best of the food.

Around Kadapa a daughter has long been counted as a cost. Families go into debt over dowries, girls are humiliated after marriage, and most women have never been told which laws are supposed to protect them. Not knowing is what keeps them stuck.

So the bulk of our advocacy is outreach and teaching, aimed mostly at people young enough to change their minds.

Not only with families, either. A great deal of it has gone into the people a village actually defers to: police officers, district officials, sarpanches, priests. When the man who performs the wedding has thought properly about the age of the bride, that reaches further than any single house call.

How the work unfolded

How this went from one woman knocking on doors to a district-wide network of women who now run it themselves.

1
Early 2000s

One woman at a time

Before there was a project name or any money for it, Sandhya started knocking on doors. She and a small team worked through two mandals, Chennur and Chinta Komma Dinne, sitting in one front room after another with pregnant women and whoever else was home.

The conversations were specific. What a daughter is worth. Why not to book a scan to find out the sex of the child, and why not to end the pregnancy on hearing the answer. That both the scan and the abortion are punishable under the PCPNDT Act, for the doctor as much as the family. That hitting your wife is a crime. That every girl should be in school, and that marrying one off as a child is illegal.

It was slow, and it was one house at a time. But in those two mandals the child sex ratio held while the district's fell.

  • Chennur — 1001 girls : 1000 boys
  • C.K. Dinne — 998 : 1000
2
2011

The census that named the problem

Then the 2011 Census came out. Across Kadapa district there were 918 girls for every 1,000 boys. That gap is thousands of girls who should have been there and were not.

It put a figure on what the door-to-door visits had been running into all along. Not a run of individual tragedies, but a pattern across the whole district, and far too big for a small team on foot.

3
2015 – 2017

Mana Bidda — “Our Child”

Mana Bidda was the three-year, EU-funded answer to that figure. It stepped into cases as they came up, pregnancies at risk and weddings being arranged for children, but the real target was further upstream.

Seven strands ran at once: baseline assessments, village meetings, sensitisation sessions, leadership training, volunteer recruitment, advocacy and psychosocial support. The ones that did the most work were held in schools with boys, on role reversal, and were led by Aarti's own girls. For most of those boys it was the first time anyone had asked them to picture it from the other side.

By the end of it, Kadapa's girl-child ratio had gone from 918 to 936 girls per 1,000 boys.

  • 1,215 village meetings
  • 105,000 participants
  • 119 abortions stopped
  • Unregistered pregnancies: 551 → 8
  • 51 counsellors trained

4
2018 – 2020

Abhaya — “Fearless”

Mana Bidda ended and the question was what would hold once the money stopped. The women who had stood up in their own villages, the ones who reported a scan or stopped a wedding, were now known for it. Being known for it is dangerous.

Abhaya was built to protect them. It put those women into coalitions so they were not standing alone, and set up support behind them: physical, psychological, social, legal. It also gave the work a permanent address in the Open Resource Centres, which any woman can still walk into. And it produced an Android app, so that knowing your rights stopped depending on somebody turning up at your door to explain them.

Open Resource Centres

Help, whenever it’s needed

There are seven Open Resource Centres, with a bigger one going up in Kadapa. Any woman can walk in for social, legal or emotional help. We do not ask about caste, religion, age or marital status.

They run counselling and a safe house, and they teach livelihood skills and leadership on the same premises.

Revati’s story

Revati arrived at Aarti Home in 2002 to learn tailoring. She is now a counsellor in Bayanapalli, a small village in Kadapa district.

She argues, locally and often, that women should work and girls should be in school. Dowry is the one she has made her own.
